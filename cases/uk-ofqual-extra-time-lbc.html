MajorUnited Kingdom·Education & society

Extra-exam-time gaming debate still cites withdrawn Ofqual counts

LBC

Why it matters

The old count is roughly 46–105% above the replacement range and helped frame a public debate about examination fairness, SEND support and alleged gaming by private schools. Retaining it makes an acknowledged reporting problem look like evidence about the scale and fairness of actual exam provision.

Still says

19 January 2025 · page labels an update on 10 February 2026
Ofqual, the exam regulator, says there are 420,000 pupils receiving extra time in exams.

Opening paragraphs · Open the source ↗

LBC reports that almost a third of pupils receive extra exam time, citing 420,000 pupils and a 42% private-school versus 27% state-school split, alongside suggestions that schools may be gaming the system.

What changed

Withdrawn17 July 2025 · replacement 27 November 2025

Ofqual withdrew its 2014–2024 access-arrangements statistics after finding substantial overstatement caused by duplicate applications and arrangements for students not taking exams that year. Its November replacement estimates 204,480–287,760 students approved for 25% extra time in 2023/24, or 14.7–20.6%, compared with the withdrawn 419,960 and 30.1%.

The replacement is a range reflecting unresolved record-linkage and duplicate uncertainty, not a single precise count. Figures describe approved arrangements, not verified use in examinations. Ofqual found no evidence of misuse or systemic failure and said students' access arrangements remained appropriate and valid. The corrected overall figures above compare the same 2023/24 year. The replacement's 2024/25 independent-school range must not be substituted for the old 2023/24 private-school percentage. This case does not establish that all private/state disparities vanished, that LBC itself accused schools of proven abuse, or that the statistics changed government policy. Retrievals show inconsistent updated labels: our first full text displayed 19 January 2025, while root independently retrieved a full page displaying 10 February 2026. That later label does not establish a substantive editorial review after withdrawal. The current page displays an update date of 10 February 2026, but this does not establish what changed then or knowledge of the withdrawal.

How the claim connects to the study

LBC explicitly attributes the count to Ofqual. Its rounded 420,000 and approximately one-third figures match the original 2023/24 release's 419,960 and 30.1%; the private/state figures match rounded 41.8% and 26.5%. The exact government release is now marked withdrawn and links the methodological explanation. The formal 17 July withdrawal covers that release, and the 27 November 2025 replacement explicitly provides revised 2023/24 values. The retrieved live LBC body retains the old counts without a withdrawal or correction warning.
